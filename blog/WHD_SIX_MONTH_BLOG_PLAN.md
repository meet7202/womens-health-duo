Publish two substantial, clinically reviewed article releases per week: Dr. Zalak Shah (PT) on Tuesdays and Dr. Charmi Shah on Fridays, at 09:00 IST as a practical operating convention. Start on 24 November 2026 and continue through 21 May 2027. This provides 52 releases over 26 weeks, with one article per doctor each week. The programme contains **39 substantial refreshes of existing guide URLs and 13 proposed new articles**. Your live article directory already lists 100 guides. Improving an existing page is the right publication action when its intent matches the new brief. Creating a second URL with a slightly different title would split the same topic across competing pages. A refreshed article can be featured again in the Learn directory and social channels, with its genuine original publication date retained and an accurate substantive-update date added.

The calendar is a production recommendation, not an assertion that two posts a week or 09:00 improves rankings. Keep a two-week approved buffer once the launch sprint is complete. If clinical review cannot keep up, publish one approved article per week and re-date the remaining slots; never fill a gap with an unreviewed health article. The Friday slots on 25 December and 1 January need approval in advance, or a deliberate date change by the editor.

**Main goal:** help a reader understand their question, recognize the limits of self-assessment, and choose a suitable next step with the appropriate clinician. Track qualified consultation interest alongside readership. A WhatsApp click is an expression of interest, not a completed appointment.

## 5. Article size and the reusable writing structure

There is no Google-preferred word count. The per-article ranges below are drafting budgets chosen for scope and readability. Most are approximately 900–1,800 words; a more complex explanation may need around 1,900. Count the main editorial body, not navigation, footer or repeated boilerplate. Stop when the question is answered well. Do not add generic FAQs or repeat definitions to reach a minimum.

### Expand the existing guides into readable, illustrated blog articles

Yes: the content of the existing guide pages should change substantially when the current page does not fully answer the question. Keep each canonical URL and develop its body into a detailed, doctor-led article with an engaging introduction, useful explanations, illustrations, sources and a clear next step. The page can look and read like a blog while remaining at its established address. Preserve genuinely useful existing content and check existing search queries before changing the page's intent.

Use storytelling to help a reader recognize a question or decision, not to manufacture medical authority. Each of the 52 briefs now has its own storytelling direction. A typical flow is **direct answer → short illustrative situation → explanation with a visual → assessment/options and limitations → practical next step**. A short scene of approximately 100–180 words is usually enough and must fit inside the article's word budget. Urgent help-seeking information comes before a story when the topic requires it.

An invented scene must be clearly labeled “Illustrative example” and must not imply a real patient, a consultation with either doctor, a diagnosis or a successful treatment outcome. Real experiences need appropriate consent and verification; a composite is not automatically anonymous. The doctors' own first-person explanations or quotations require their actual contribution and approval. End by resolving the reader's information need, not by inventing a happy clinical ending.

For example, `/what-is-diastasis-recti/` can become a richer article with a brief illustrative reader concern, a clinician-reviewed abdominal-wall diagram, an explanation of the term, what an assessment considers, limits of self-checking, links to recovery/core guides, and an appropriate consultation option. It should retain its existing URL and acquire a truthful substantive-update date after the revision is approved.

Use this structure flexibly:

1. One clear H1 that matches the patient's question. Use the brief's title as the default H1.
2. A direct, clinician-approved answer of roughly 40–80 words. State important limits immediately. Do not put a long promotional introduction before the answer.
3. Visible, truthful author/reviewer identity and actual dates. Add a short table of contents when it improves navigation.
4. The article-specific sections listed in the brief. Allocate roughly 15% to explanation/context, 35% to the main decision or assessment, 20% to practical preparation, 15% to exceptions/help-seeking, and 15% to concise related questions and next steps. These are editable planning proportions, not quotas.
5. One or two useful visuals placed next to the explanation they support. Include the same essential information in text for accessibility.
6. A topic-specific “When to seek care” section, approved by the responsible clinician. Urgent symptoms must not lead to a routine WhatsApp booking CTA.
7. A relevant next step: assessment, a question checklist, a service page, or a routine consultation link. Avoid repeated sales banners in every section.
8. Two to four genuinely useful follow-up questions only where they add information not already answered. Do not repeat every heading as an FAQ.
9. References linked close to material claims, plus a compact source list and a correction route/editorial policy.

Write a separate concise SEO title from the H1 if needed. Put the main topic early and make it accurate and distinctive. Around 50–60 characters is a preview convenience, not an eligibility rule. Descriptions should summarize the actual article in natural language; use about 140–160 characters under the current repository policy when that fits. The repository applies title/description caps and can append CTA copy, so inspect the final rendered result rather than assuming the source string survives unchanged. If reviewed metadata needs a different policy, revise the helpers and their checks together. No character limit guarantees display. Do not force a brand suffix or repeated keyword into every heading. Alt text describes the image rather than targeting a keyword density.
